So far, we have treated the aberration function $\chi(\bm{k})$ as a phase shift applied to each tilted planewave contributing to the converged probe.
However, this phase surface also has a direct geometric interpretation closely related to the ray diagrams encountered earlier in the class.

Recall that a tilted planewave with wavevector $\bm{k}$ propagates at an angle relative to the optic axis.
If neighboring wavevectors acquire different phases, the local slope of the aberration surface determines how the corresponding rays are deflected in real space.
Mathematically, this local ray displacement is proportional to the gradient of the aberration function:

:::{math}
:label: aberration_gradient_eq
\Delta \bm{r}(\bm{k}) \propto \nabla_{\bm{k}} \chi(\bm{k}).
:::

This relationship is a direct manifestation of Fourier duality:
a phase ramp in reciprocal space corresponds to a translation in real space.
The steeper the local slope of the aberration surface, the larger the displacement of the corresponding electron rays.

## Geometric Interpretation of Aberrations

Importantly, this provides the connection between the wave-optical and geometric-optical descriptions of aberrations:
- The aberration surface $\chi(\bm{k})$ describes the coherent phase error of the electron wave.
- The gradient $\nabla \chi(\bm{k})$ describes the local ray deflection.
- The resulting displacement field produces geometric image distortions.

In this sense, geometric ray optics emerges naturally as the local-gradient approximation of coherent wave optics.

The center panel shows the corresponding displacement field obtained from the gradient of the aberration surface, $\nabla \chi$.
Finally, the right panel applies this displacement field to a checkerboard tracer image, visualizing the resulting geometric distortion.

Notice that different aberrations generate characteristic distortion patterns:
- Defocus produces a radially symmetric expansion or contraction.
- Two-fold astigmatism stretches the image preferentially along one direction.
- Coma introduces an asymmetric displacement field resembling a skew or shear.

These geometric distortions are directly related to the ray diagrams commonly used in geometric optics.
However, unlike ray optics, the wave-optical framework also captures interference and diffraction effects, which become essential at atomic resolution.
