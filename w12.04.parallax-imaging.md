In the previous section, we saw that a Ronchigram contains information about the local gradient of the aberration surface.
Modern phase-sensitive imaging methods exploit this relationship computationally by treating different regions of the Ronchigram as independent tilted illuminations.

One particularly intuitive example is parallax imaging, which forms virtual images from subsets of the diffraction pattern and computationally aligns them using the aberration-induced displacement field.

Parallax imaging provides a direct bridge between:

- geometric ray optics,
- Ronchigram distortions,
- and modern computational phase retrieval.

Importantly, it demonstrates how aberration correction can be performed numerically after acquisition.

## Reciprocity and Virtual Bright-Field Images

Recall that each point within the probe-forming aperture corresponds to a tilted planewave illumination.
Equivalently, selecting a small region of the Ronchigram isolates a subset of these tilted illuminations.

Using a pixelated detector, we can therefore construct virtual detectors after acquisition.
For example, selecting a small detector region centered at reciprocal coordinate $\bm{k}$ produces a virtual bright-field (vBF) image corresponding to that illumination angle.

By reciprocity, this is equivalent to illuminating the specimen with a tilted probe.
As we saw previously, a local phase gradient produces a real-space displacement:

:::{math}
:label: parallax_shift_eq
\Delta \bm{r}(\bm{k})
\propto
\nabla_{\bm{k}} \chi(\bm{k}).
:::

Thus, different regions of the Ronchigram generate laterally shifted images of the specimen.

Notice that:

- detector regions on opposite sides of the aperture produce opposite image shifts,
- larger detector angles produce larger shifts,
- and increasing defocus increases the displacement magnitude approximately linearly.

This is precisely the geometric-optics picture of defocus:
electrons with larger illumination angles intersect the image plane at increasingly displaced positions.

## Computational Alignment

If the displacement field is known, we can computationally undo these shifts and align the virtual bright-field images before summation.

Without alignment, summing all detector pixels simply produces an incoherent bright-field image:

:::{math}
:label: incoherent_bf_eq
I_{\mathrm{BF}}(\bm{r})
=
\sum_{\bm{k}}
I(\bm{r},\bm{k}),
:::

where $I(\bm{r},\bm{k})$ is the intensity measured at scan position $\bm{r}$ and detector coordinate $\bm{k}$.

Because different detector angles correspond to shifted images, this incoherent summation introduces blur.
However, if we compensate for the aberration-induced displacement before summation, the individual views add coherently.

:::

In [](#parallax_alignment_widget), the shifted virtual bright-field images are cumulatively aligned before summation.
Notice that computational alignment:

- sharpens fine features,
- increases contrast transfer,
- and compensates for residual aberrations numerically.

This is the fundamental idea underlying parallax reconstruction methods.

## Fourier Interpretation

The alignment procedure can also be understood in Fourier space.
Each detector coordinate contributes a shifted phase factor determined by the aberration gradient:

:::{math}
:label: parallax_phase_eq
\phi_{\mathrm{prlx}}(\bm{q})
=
\sum_{\bm{k}: |\bm{k}| < k_0}
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}
\nabla_{\bm{k}}\chi(\bm{k}) \cdot \bm{q}}
\;
G(\bm{q},\bm{k}),
:::

where:

- $\bm{q}$ is the image spatial frequency,
- $G(\bm{q},\bm{k})$ is the Fourier transform of the detector-resolved image intensity,
- and the exponential factor compensates for the aberration-induced displacement associated with detector coordinate $\bm{k}$.

This expression illustrates an important conceptual point:
parallax reconstruction is fundamentally a phase-alignment problem.
The goal is to estimate and compensate the relative phase shifts between different detector angles before combining information.
