In practice, the residual aberrations of a STEM probe are not determined by directly inspecting the probe wavefunction itself.
Instead, aberrations are commonly diagnosed experimentally using a Ronchigram (also called a diffractogram), which provides a direct visualization of the local phase distortions across the probe-forming aperture.

Ronchigrams are central to modern aberration-corrected electron microscopy because they provide immediate visual feedback about residual aberrations and allow microscope operators to iteratively tune the optical system toward an optimal probe condition.

## Ronchigram Formation

To form a Ronchigram, we illuminate a thin amorphous or weakly scattering specimen with a highly converged probe and record the resulting diffraction pattern on a pixelated detector @10.1017/S1551929522001043.

In the absence of a specimen, the diffraction pattern simply corresponds to the probe-forming aperture:

:::{math}
:label: ronchigram_empty_eq
I(\bm{k}) = |\psi(\bm{k})|^2.
:::

However, when the probe interacts with a specimen having a spatially varying transmission function

:::{math}
:label: transmission_function_eq
T(\bm{r}) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\sigma V(\bm{r})},
:::

the outgoing wave becomes

:::{math}
:label: exit_wave_eq
\psi_{\mathrm{exit}}(\bm{r})
=
\psi(\bm{r}) \; T(\bm{r}),
:::

and the measured Ronchigram intensity is

:::{math}
:label: ronchigram_eq
I(\bm{k})
=
\left|
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{r}\rightarrow\bm{k}}
\left[
\psi_{\mathrm{exit}}(\bm{r})
\right]
\right|^2.
:::

Here, $V(\bm{r})$ is the specimen [electrostatic potential](xref:cbl-apmc/scattering-potentials) and $\sigma$ is the interaction parameter.

Because the specimen introduces local phase modulations, different regions of the probe interfere in the diffraction plane.
The resulting Ronchigram therefore contains information about the local phase curvature of the probe aberration function.

## Information Transfer

The key idea behind Ronchigram interpretation is that regions of the aperture with slowly varying aberration phase transfer information coherently, while regions with rapidly varying phase distort the probe and reduce transfer.

Recall from the previous section that the gradient of the aberration surface determines the local ray displacement:

:::{math}
:label: gradient_displacement_repeat_eq
\Delta \bm{r}(\bm{k})
\propto
\nabla_{\bm{k}} \chi(\bm{k}).
:::

Equally important is the curvature of the aberration surface.
Regions where the phase surface bends strongly correspond to rapidly changing ray displacements, producing local magnification distortions and probe delocalization.

As a result:

- Flat regions of the aberration surface produce nearly undistorted transfer.
- Highly curved regions produce image blur and geometric distortion.
- Caustics and folds in the displacement field lead to strong Ronchigram distortions.

Experimentally, this means that the usable angular range of the probe can often be identified directly from the Ronchigram as the region where the contrast appears smooth and interpretable.

Different aberrations produce characteristic distortions in the Ronchigram pattern.

In @ronchigram_widget, the left panel shows the phase structure of the probe contrast-transfer function (CTF), while the right panel shows the resulting Ronchigram intensity after interacting with a weak random phase object.

Try adjusting the aberration coefficients and observe how the Ronchigram changes:

- Defocus produces concentric magnification changes and symmetric ring distortions.
- Astigmatism stretches the Ronchigram preferentially along one direction.
- Coma produces strongly asymmetric distortions and local shearing.
- Higher-order aberrations introduce increasingly complex folding and warping patterns.

Notice that the Ronchigram often appears visually smoother near the center of the aperture.
This corresponds to the low-angle region where the aberration phase varies slowly.
Toward larger scattering angles, the aberration phase oscillates more rapidly and the Ronchigram becomes increasingly distorted.

### Magnification and Caustics

An especially important feature of Ronchigrams is the appearance of bright caustic lines or folds.
These arise when neighboring rays overlap due to local changes in magnification.

Geometrically, the local magnification is determined by the Jacobian of the displacement field:

:::{math}
:label: jacobian_eq
\mathbf{J}
=
\frac{\partial \Delta \bm{r}}{\partial \bm{k}}
\propto
\frac{\partial^2 \chi}{\partial \bm{k}^2}.
:::

When the determinant of this Jacobian approaches zero, the local magnification diverges and rays accumulate into bright caustic features.
These caustics are extremely sensitive to residual aberrations and therefore provide a powerful diagnostic for aberration correction.

### Aberration Correction

Modern aberration-corrected STEM instruments iteratively tune multipole corrector elements to flatten the aberration surface over the largest possible angular range.

From the Ronchigram perspective, the goal is to maximize the region of smooth, interpretable contrast while eliminating strong distortions and caustics.
Equivalently, from the wave-optical perspective, the goal is to minimize the phase variation of the aberration function within the probe-forming aperture.
This phase-tolerance criterion ensures that all contributing planewaves interfere constructively at the probe position.

As aberration correction improves, larger convergence semiangles can be used while maintaining a tightly focused probe.
This directly improves the achievable real-space resolution.

Historically, Ronchigrams were viewed directly on fluorescent viewing screens during manual microscope alignment.
Today, pixelated detectors allow Ronchigrams to be recorded quantitatively as part of modern 4D-STEM experiments.

In fact, many modern phase-retrieval and ptychographic methods can be understood as using the full information content of Ronchigrams recorded at every probe position.
Rather than treating Ronchigrams solely as alignment tools, modern computational microscopy interprets them as rich measurements encoding both specimen structure and probe aberrations simultaneously.
